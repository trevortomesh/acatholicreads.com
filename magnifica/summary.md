# Magnifica Humanitas — Summary

**Pope Leo XIV, *Magnifica Humanitas: On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence*.** His first encyclical. Signed May 15, 2026 — the 135th anniversary of Leo XIII's *Rerum Novarum* — and published May 25, 2026. 245 numbered paragraphs: an introduction, five chapters, and a conclusion, addressed to all people of good will.

**The argument in one sentence:** Technology is not the enemy and never neutral — the question is whether we build **Babel** (power pursuing domination, humanity made uniform) or rebuild **Jerusalem** with Nehemiah (shared responsibility, God at the center, dignity restored piece by piece) — and every use of AI should be judged by whether it safeguards the human person and makes human life more human.

---

## Introduction — Babel or Jerusalem (§§1–16)

Humanity faces "a pivotal choice: either to construct a new Tower of Babel or to build the city in which God and humanity dwell together" (§1). The "new things" of our age are digitalization, artificial intelligence, and robotics (§§3–4). Technology is not antagonistic to humanity, but its power is now largely private and transnational, which makes it harder to direct toward the common good (§§4–5). Babel is pride and uniformity — "a single language, a single technology, a single direction" (§7); Nehemiah's rebuilding is shared work with God at the center (§8). Technology "is never neutral" in practice, because "it takes on the characteristics of those who devise, finance, regulate and use it" (§9). The call: "remain profoundly human" — builders of communion, not architects of Babel (§§15–16).

## Chapter One — A Dynamic Approach Faithful to the Gospel (§§17–45)

The Church's Social Doctrine is a living tradition, not a static code. Truth is "a good to be shared," not a territory to defend (§25), and the doctrine is "not a handbook of principles and norms to be applied, but a process of shared discernment" (§27). The chapter retraces the tradition: *Rerum Novarum* (§30), *Quadragesimo Anno* and subsidiarity (§31), Pius XII (§§29, 32), John XXIII (§33), Vatican II (§34), Paul VI — development as "the new name for peace" (§§35–36), John Paul II (§§37–39), Benedict XVI's *Caritas in Veritate* (§§40–41), and Francis (§§42–44).

## Chapter Two — Foundations and Principles of the Social Doctrine (§§46–89)

The person is created in God's image, for communion (§§48–50). Human dignity "does not depend on a person's abilities, wealth or position in life" — it is "a gift that precedes and transcends each person" (§50), and the ideology that worth must be earned is "particularly insidious" (§51). **Ontological dignity** belongs to every person "simply by virtue of existing" and cannot be diminished by sin, failure, or exclusion (§52); with *Dignitas Infinita*, it is infinite (§53). The first human right is the right to life, from conception to natural end (§§54–55).

Six principles follow: the **common good**, "the social expression of the dignity recognized in every person" (§59); the **universal destination of goods**, which outranks private property and now includes patents, algorithms, platforms, and data (§§66–67); **subsidiarity**, whose digital "highest level" is the major technology platforms, not the State (§§68–72); **solidarity**, a principle and a virtue (§§73–75); **social justice**, confronting "structures of sin," with migrants as its litmus test (§§77–81); and **integral human development** — does technology make people "more humane and fraternal"? (§§82–85). The chapter ends with an examination of conscience for the Church itself (§§86–89).

## Chapter Three — Technology and Dominance: The Grandeur of Humanity in Light of the Promises of AI (§§90–130)

The technocratic paradigm — "the logic of efficiency, control and profit alone" — spreads with AI (§§92–93), while control of platforms, data, and computing power rests with major economic actors (§95). AI systems are "more 'cultivated' than 'built'" (§98). So-called AIs "do not undergo experiences, do not possess a body, do not feel joy or pain… Nor do they have a moral conscience"; their learning is "a form of statistical adaptation" that "does not imply inner growth" (§99). Three cautions for everyday use (§100): the ease of results, the impression of objectivity, and the simulation of human communication. AI is "not… morally neutral," since "every technical tool embodies choices and priorities" (§104); responsibility for its decisions must be clearly identifiable (§105); "a more moral AI is not enough if that morality is determined by a few" (§107); data should be managed as a common good (§108); and AI must be "disarmed," freed from monopolistic control (§110), with a direct appeal to its developers (§111).

Against transhumanism (§§115–128): "humanity flourishes not despite limitations, but often through them" (§118); the authentic "more than human" is grace, for "an error can be a catalyst for profound change" in a person in a way it never can be for an algorithm (§128). The test is John Paul II's question: does it make human life "more human"? (§129). The chapter closes with Augustine's two cities, built by two loves (§130).

## Chapter Four — Safeguarding Humanity at a Time of Transformation: Truth, Work, Freedom (§§131–181)

**Truth** is a common good, threatened by AI-amplified disinformation (§§132–134); the encyclical calls for an "ecology of communication" (§137) and an educational alliance that teaches people "to decide when and for what purpose" AI "ought not to be used" (§140), with protections for minors (§§141–142). **Work** is "the essential key" to the social question (§148); profit cannot justify systematically sacrificing jobs, "because the human person is an end, not a means" (§152); development needs measures of dignity alongside GDP (§159). **Freedom** is threatened by the digital attention economy, "designed to capture users' time and attention, exploiting their vulnerabilities and weakening their inner freedom" (§170); by social control through data (§171); and by new forms of slavery behind the digital economy — data labelers, content moderators, children mining rare earths (§§173–175). The Pope asks pardon for the Church's long delay in condemning slavery (§176) and names the extraction of health and genetic data a new colonialism (§178); the person must never be reduced to a data point (§180).

## Chapter Five — The Culture of Power and the Civilization of Love (§§182–228)

Against a culture of power that normalizes war (§§188–192), the encyclical sets Paul VI's "civilization of love" (§§186–187). The "just war" theory is called "now outdated" (§192) and nuclear deterrence an "erroneous belief" (§194). On AI weapons: "it is not permissible to entrust lethal or otherwise irreversible decisions to artificial systems," and "no algorithm can make war morally acceptable" (§198) — with criteria of personal responsibility, a moral timeframe, and protection of civilians (§199), and requirements of retraceable decisions, effective human control of lethal force, and an international framework (§200). The chapter closes with five paths everyone can walk toward peace (§§213–227): disarming words, peace through justice, the perspective of victims, healthy realism, and revived dialogue — a "culture of negotiation" in place of the culture of power (§221).

## Conclusion — A Program of Christian Life (§§229–245)

"Let each builder choose with care how to build" (1 Cor 3:10, §229). Four pillars: **faith** in the Incarnation (§§230–233) — "No computational system, however sophisticated, can create a heart that gives itself, or a conscience that discerns good from evil" (§233); **love**, a Eucharistic spirituality of unity (§§234–235); **hope**, the wise architect building for the common good (§§236–240), with Nehemiah as the parable of our vocation (§241) and the New Jerusalem as gift (§242); and **prayer**, ending with Mary's *Magnificat* (§§243–245).
